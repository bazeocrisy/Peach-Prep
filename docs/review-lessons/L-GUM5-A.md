## MUST
- [SK-GUM5-3-P2, slot 0] "All the {0} jackets were piled on a bench..." Nothing in the prompt or the sentence says the jackets belong to more than one student. The only place that says so is the clue ("Both blanks show more than one owner"). "All the student's jackets" is grammatical and makes sense: all of one student's jackets. A careful adult could defend student's, so the item has two defensible answers. Learn 1 and SK-GUM5-3-T4 handle the same problem by stating the owners. Fix: make the plural explicit in the sentence: "All twenty {0} jackets were piled on a bench next to the {1} garden." Or add a stem line: "(The jackets belong to many students. The garden belongs to many children.)"

## SHOULD
- [GUM5-1 Teach example; SK-GUM5-1-P3, SK-GUM5-1-T1, SK-GUM5-1-T3, SK-GUM5-1-T5] Several sentences put day and street abbreviations in running prose and present them as correct: "on Mon., Jan. 12", "closed on Sun., but it opens on Mon.", "on Fri. after lunch", "moved to Tues. because of rain", "on Oak St." Standard American usage spells out days, and usually streets, in a sentence. Abbreviations belong in dates on a heading, in addresses, on signs and in schedules. Most at risk is T5, where the keyed "correct" sentence depends on "Tues." in prose. A teacher could call it incorrect, which leaves the child's picture of the rule muddled. No key is wrong on capitalization alone, so this is SHOULD. Fix: put the abbreviations where they belong, for example "The note said: Book fair, Tues., Oct. 6" or "Our address is 12 Oak St." Or turn T5's key into a sentence with no abbreviation (for example "Dr. Ali gave our class a Korean fan.") and test abbreviations in a list or address.
- [GUM5-5 Teach, point 1 and `words` "clause"] A clause is defined as "a naming part and an action part". The lesson's own clauses then include "the bus was late", "it was raining", "the sky was full of clouds" and "the show was funny", which have no action. A rule-following child is told these are clauses even though they fail the definition. Fix: "a naming part and a telling part (what someone or something does or is)."
- [GUM5-5 Teach, point 2; SK-GUM5-5-P5 clue and why] "If tells what has to happen first" describes time order, but if sets a condition. A child can then confuse if with before or after, which the same point says "tell when". Fix: "If tells what must happen for the other part to happen." Change the P5 clue and why to match: "Which word tells what must happen for the picnic to move inside?"
- [SK-GUM5-1-P4 clue; SK-GUM5-3-P5 clue] Each clue fits exactly one choice. In P4, "Look for a word made from the name of a place" matches only italian. In P5, "Look for a plural that does not end in s" matches only mens'. These are close to giveaways for Hint 1. Fix: P4: "Check each word. Is it a title before a name, or a word made from the name of a place?" P5: "For each owner word, decide if it is plural. Then check where the apostrophe goes."
- [GUM5-5 Test vs Practice] SK-GUM5-5-T4 copies the structure of SK-GUM5-5-P4: the same subject is repeated with because versus a time word (Sam smiled / The dog barked). SK-GUM5-5-T5 copies P3: a fragment plus a sentence that completes it. The sentences differ, but the item design is identical, so the test checks a memorized pattern more than the skill. Fix: change T4 to a dropdown or mc that asks for the right joining word in a new context (for example although or after), or make T5 an ms that asks for the two fragments.

## NIT
- [GUM5-1 Teach, point 3] "They always start with a capital" overstates the rule. Some conventional uses are lowercase, such as french fries and china plates. Fix: drop "always", or say "almost always".
- [GUM5-1 Teach, point 1; SK-GUM5-1-P4 clue] "When no name comes after it, a word like mayor or doctor stays lowercase" and "uncle gets a capital only when a name comes right after it" are both false for direct address ("Thank you, Doctor." "Hi, Uncle!"). Fix: "usually stays lowercase." Also, uncle is a family word, not a formal title. Consider replacing that choice with a common noun.
- [SK-GUM5-2-T2] "{0} please feed the fish. {1} I will feed them right now." reads as one speaker giving an order and then answering it. Fix: give the reply a frame, for example "{1} I will feed them right now, Mom."
- [SK-GUM5-3-T2] "My two puppy's bowls" can be argued as "my puppy's two bowls", but the reading is strained and most adults would reject it. Optional fix: "My two {0} bowls" → "Both of my {0} bowls".
- [SK-GUM5-4-T5] The distractor Several is never taught. A child can still choose Each, but this tests a word outside the lesson. Fix: replace Several with "All", or add several to Teach point 2.
- [GUM5-6 Teach, big idea] "Put a comma after that part" is stated with no exception. A short opening phrase ("After lunch we played") often takes no comma. No item marks a no-comma short phrase wrong, so no key is affected. Fix: add "usually" or "it is always OK to".
- [GUM5-7 Teach, point 1] The small-word list (a, an, the, and, of, in, on, to) leaves out for and at. Items depend on both: the example "A Song for the Rain", "A Gift for Grandpa" and SK-GUM5-7-P5's why ("at and the are small words"). Fix: add for and at to the list.
- [Test clues: SK-GUM5-5-T2, SK-GUM5-3-T5, SK-GUM5-1-T4] Each clue points to a single choice: "the word that tells about time" (only until), "a number word" (only "two friend's"), "Look for a title" (only governor). These show only after submission, so the impact is small. Fix: use general strategy wording, as above.
