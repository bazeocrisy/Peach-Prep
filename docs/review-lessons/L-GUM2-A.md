## MUST
- [SK-GUM2-6-P4 clue] The clue makes a false grammar claim: "A pronoun at the start of a sentence, before the verb, must be a subject pronoun." Possessives break this all the time: "My dog barked." "Her book fell." "Mine is the blue one." The item's own key sentence has one too: "his" in "Him and his sister walk..." comes before the verb and is not a subject pronoun. The clue also nearly gives away the answer. Only two options start with a pronoun ("Him and..." and "They invited..."), and "They" is a subject pronoun, so the clue leaves only one choice. Fix: "Find the pronoun that does the action. If it does the action, it must be a subject pronoun like I, he, she, we or they."

## SHOULD
- [SK-GUM2-2-P2 clue] The clue gives both deciding facts: "Boots means more than one... they are close by." The Teach maps "more than one + close by" straight to "these", so the clue all but names the answer. Fix: "Is the noun one thing or more than one? Are the boots close by or far away?"
- [SK-GUM2-3-P3 clue] "The big words in a holiday's name start with capitals. Small words like of stay small." Only one option fits that rule exactly, so the clue answers the item. Fix: "This is the name of a holiday. Which words in a holiday's name are big, important words?"
- [SK-GUM2-7-P3 clue] "Bedtime is a time, not a place. Which word tells when?" Only one option (before) tells when, so the clue picks it. The underlying problem is distractor design: each distractor differs from the key only by its where/when category. Fix: ask "Is bedtime a place or a time?" Or add a second when-word distractor that does not make sense, such as "until" ("We brush our teeth until bedtime" is odd but tells when). Then the child has to picture the meaning.
- [SK-GUM2-8-P1 clue] "The comma goes between two numbers, not between the month and a number." Only one option has a comma between two numbers, so the clue gives the answer. Fix: "Where does the comma go in a date? Look at the example in the lesson."
- [GUM2-7 Teach example note and Learn 2 step 1] "Each preposition comes right before a noun" and "Look for a small word that comes right before a noun" are not true of the examples given: "under her bed", "during the fire drill". A child who follows the rule literally may pick "the" or "fire" (the word right before "drill"). Fix: "A preposition comes before a noun, often with a word like the or her in between: under her bed, during the fire drill." In Learn 2, drop "right".
- [GUM2-8 Learn 1 step 1] Step 1 writes the list as "beans, peas and carrots" with no serial comma. The steps and the answer then require "beans, peas, and carrots". A child sees two different punctuations of the same list in one worked example. Fix: write step 1 as "The class grew three things: beans, peas, and carrots." (No item tests the serial comma alone, which is good. Please keep it that way, since both styles are accepted.)
- [GUM2-6 Teach point 3 vs GUM2-2 Teach point 3] The same file teaches my/your/his/her/its/our/their two ways. GUM2-2 treats them as determiners, matching the standard's term "possessive adjectives". GUM2-6 calls the same words "possessive pronouns". The brief says to use the Georgia chart's terms, and the chart files these words under determiners (possessive adjectives). Fix: in GUM2-6, say "Owning words tell who owns something: my, your, his, her, its, our, their come before a noun. Mine, yours, his, hers, ours and theirs stand alone." Do not label the first group "possessive pronouns".

## NIT
- [GUM2-6 Teach example] "Jada gave Leo a book" becomes "She gave him her book". The rewrite changes "a book" to "her book" and adds a new owner, so it is not a pure pronoun swap. Consider "Jada gave Leo her book." → "She gave him her book."
- [SK-GUM2-1-P4 clue] "A verb with no ending ... shows the present" is also true of the plain verb inside "will lock". It works only because the clue checks "will" first. Consider "If there is no will, a verb with no ending or with -s shows the present."
- [SK-GUM2-5-T3 clue] "A plural never needs an apostrophe" is a slight overstatement (for example, "dot your i's"). It is harmless at grade 3, but "Plural words like these do not need an apostrophe" is safer. This is a Test item, so the clue may not be shown.
- [GUM2-8 scope] The standard text names "addresses". The lesson covers only city and state, which matches the site's plain wording. Consider adding one Teach line with a street address ("We live at 12 Oak Lane, Macon, Georgia").
