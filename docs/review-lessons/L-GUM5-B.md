## SHOULD
- [GUM5-1 Teach example; SK-GUM5-1-P3; SK-GUM5-1-T3; SK-GUM5-1-T5] Days and months are abbreviated inside ordinary sentences: "on Mon., Jan. 12", "closed on Sun., but it opens on Mon.", "on Fri. after lunch", "moved to Tues. because of rain". Standard American usage spells out days and months in running prose and saves the short forms for calendars, lists, signs and addresses. T5 marks "Our soccer game moved to Tues. because of rain." as a *correct* sentence, so the child is taught that this is good writing. A careful adult could say it is not, although the item is still answerable because the three distractors are clearly wrong. Fix: put day and month abbreviations where they are normal (a calendar note, a sign, a date line such as "Mon., Jan. 12" by itself, or a list), or swap them for street and title abbreviations in the sentences. For T5, replace the Tues. key with something like "We mailed the letter to 14 Pine St." or "Ms. Diaz is our new coach."
- [GUM5-5 Teach point 2; SK-GUM5-5-P5 clue and why] "If tells what has to happen first" is misleading. *If* introduces a condition, not something earlier in time ("I will be sad if you leave" has nothing that happens first). Calling it a time word also blurs it with *before* and *after*, which the same point lists as time words. P5's clue ("Which word tells what has to happen first?") repeats the Teach definition of *if* almost word for word, so the hint nearly hands over the answer. Fix: Teach: "If tells what must be true for the other part to happen." P5 clue: "The picnic moves inside only in one case. Which word sets up that case?" P5 why: "...if tells what must happen for the picnic to move inside."
- [SK-GUM5-7-T4] Two distractors are too obviously wrong: "Write it in all capital letters." and "Put a comma after it." Only "Put it in quotation marks" tempts a child. Fix: use choices that test the real decision, for example "Underline it." (key), "Put it in quotation marks.", "Write it in slanted letters (italics)." (wrong because she is writing by hand) and "Capitalize only the first word."
- [SK-GUM5-2-T2] The text "{0} please feed the fish. {1} I will feed them right now." puts two speakers' lines side by side with no quotation marks and no sign that the speaker changed. A child can read it as one person talking to themselves. Fix: add the context in the prompt ("Mom asks Leah to feed the fish, and Leah answers."), or split the lines: "Mom said, {0} please feed the fish.\" Leah said, \"{1} I will feed them right now.\"" If you split them, check that the quotation marks render cleanly.
- [GUM5-1 all items] Every proper adjective in Teach, Learn, Practice and Test is a nationality from a country or continent name. That fits the Teach definition, but the Teach line "describing words made from the names of places" is the only definition the child gets (see the NIT below). This is acceptable for an Introduce row. Still, the lesson never shows that a proper adjective is a describing word and not the place name itself, except through the dropdown "Cuba/Africa" options. Fix: add one Learn step or Teach sentence that contrasts the two directly: "Mexico is a place. Mexican is a describing word: Mexican food."

## NIT
- [GUM5-1 Teach point 3] Proper adjectives can also come from people's names (Victorian, Shakespearean). Saying they come from "the names of places" is a reasonable grade-3 simplification, but "made from names, like the names of places" would be more accurate.
- [GUM5-4 Teach big; SK-GUM5-4-P1 why] "Everyone ... means one at a time" / "every single person, one at a time" is a memory trick, not a meaning. *Everyone* refers to the whole group. Consider "Everyone and each are treated as ONE, so they go with is, has and likes."
- [GUM5-4 scope] The `gum` row says "correct agreement". The lesson covers only subject-verb agreement, not pronoun agreement ("Everyone brought their/his or her lunch"). That is fine for an Introduce row and avoids a usage debate. No change needed. I note it only so the lead knows the scope was chosen on purpose.
- [GUM5-6 Teach point 2] Many style guides make the comma optional after a short introductory phrase ("In the spring we planted beans"). The items wisely never treat a missing comma after a short phrase as the error; every missing-comma distractor is an introductory clause. Consider adding "usually" to point 2, to match point 3.
- [SK-GUM5-7-P1 why; SK-GUM5-7-T1 why] The why wraps the whole sentence in double quotation marks around a title that is already in double quotation marks (`"My sister read the poem "The Moon and the Fox" to me."`). That is hard to read in a lesson about quotation marks. Fix: "The answer is the sentence with "The Moon and the Fox" in quotation marks...", or use single quotes for the outer sentence.
