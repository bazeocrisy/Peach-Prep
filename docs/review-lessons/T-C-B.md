## MUST
- [SK-TC2c-P1] The prompt asks "When was this letter written?", but the box right above the letter says "This letter is historical fiction. It is made up." Read literally, the letter was written recently by a modern author, not in March 1928. An adult can fairly say the keyed answer is false, and the item models the very confusion the standard (context in which a text *was written*) should clear up. Fix: "What date did Ella write at the top of her letter?" or "When does Ella's letter take place?" (keep the key "in March 1928").

## SHOULD
- [3.T.C.2.c, framing of p1 and p2] Both passages are labeled historical fiction, so the lesson teaches each story's setting as if it were the context of writing. The standard asks the child to identify the time period, individual and situation in which a text was written. The Learn examples (1976 diary, ranger sign) do this correctly; the passages blur it. Fix: reword the boxes as framed documents, e.g. "Imagine this letter was written by a girl on a Georgia farm in 1928. Back then, most farm families did not have electricity." Then use "the writer" and "written in" consistently (also in P5's "set in 1928" and T1's "is set in"). Or add one Teach point: "Historical fiction is written today but pretends to be written long ago. Use the date and the box to learn its pretend time and place."
- [3.T.C.1.a Teach point 2] "Look for words like should, please and let's" is a keyword shortcut that p2, the test passage, trips. p2 is informational, yet it says "Please get there ten minutes early" and gives commands ("Bring…", "Wear…"). A child following Teach could call it persuasive on T1. Fix: add "Check what the whole text mostly does. Directions like 'Bring a water bottle' tell readers how to do something; they are not trying to change their minds."
- [3.T.C.1.b scope] The standard covers features in "print, digital, auditory, spoken, and visual modes", but the Teach and all items cover only print and visual features. Fix: add a Teach point on digital and audio features (a link, a menu, a play button for a sound or video, a pop-up glossary). Then add or change one Practice item, e.g. "On a website about monarchs, what does a play button next to the heading most likely do?"
- [3.T.C.1.b p2 paragraph 2; SK-TC1b-T3 Part B; SK-TC1b-T4 glossary] "the caterpillar makes a hard case called a chrysalis" is misleading. A monarch chrysalis is not something the caterpillar builds, the way a moth spins a cocoon. The caterpillar sheds its last skin, and its own body hardens into the chrysalis. Fix: "Then the caterpillar sheds its skin and becomes a chrysalis with a hard outer shell." Update the T3 Part B quote to match, and change the T4 glossary to "chrysalis: the hard-shelled stage when a caterpillar changes into a butterfly".
- [SK-TC1b-P1 clue] "A warning is a kind of message… which one is about a beaver sending a message" turns the question into matching "message" to "Talk". Only one heading is about communicating, so the clue all but names the answer. Fix: "Think about how one animal could tell others about danger. Read each heading and ask which section would explain that."
- [SK-TC1b-T1 clue] "Which heading is about something a bird could see?" points straight to "Bright Colors…", the only heading that names something visible. It also skips the section's real reason, which is taste. Fix: "Read each heading. Which section would explain why one animal stays away from another?"
- [SK-TC1c-T4] All three distractors (long word list with no pictures, very small letters, removing the picture) obviously make the poster worse, so the two keys stand out at once. Fix: use plausible but weaker changes, e.g. "Add a fact about how many kinds of frogs live in Georgia", "Change the title to the frog's science name" and "Add a paragraph about what peepers eat".
- [3.T.C.2.a scope] The standard is "Describe the perspective of a narrator or speakers and distinguish it from one's own." Much of the lesson tests first- versus third-person labels instead: Teach points 1–2, the example, Learn 1, P4 and T5. That is closer to grade-4 content, and T5 tests nothing but the label. The Teach also never mentions a poem's "speaker". Fix: replace T5 with an item on how the narrator's view differs from a reader's (for example, a short stem where a narrator calls a rainy hike "ruined" while details show fun). Add a Teach point: "In a poem, the voice is called the speaker. The speaker has a point of view too."
- [3.T.C.2.a Teach point 2; SK-TC2a-P4 clue] "Names, he, his and they mean someone outside is telling about others" is false as a general rule. First-person narrators use names and he/they all the time, as in this lesson's own p1 ("Dev is six, and he talks to everybody"). Fix: "If the narrator says I or we about themself, it is first person. If the narrator never does, and tells about characters by name or with he, she and they, it is third person."
- [SK-TC2a-T3] The distractor "The narrator yawns while Grandpa smiles at a bird" includes Grandpa smiling at the heron. That detail could itself lead a reader to see the morning as peaceful, which makes it arguable as an answer. The key is still the best choice, but cut the ambiguity. Fix: "The narrator yawns and wishes to be back in bed."
